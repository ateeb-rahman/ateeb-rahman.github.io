PROJECT 01

SA-25 Phantom: Low Speed Aileron Structural Design

Low-boom, low-drag supersonic airliner. MSc Aerospace Vehicle Design Group Design Project, Cranfield University.

March 2025 to October 2025Project Manager, structural design teamLow speed aileron designerCATIA V6 on 3DEXPERIENCE, NASTRAN/PATRAN, AFGROW

The aircraft and my role

The SA-25 Phantom is a 100-passenger supersonic airliner designed with low drag and a reduced sonic boom in mind. It continues a line of Cranfield supersonic design projects and was developed by a team of twelve, split into a structures subgroup of seven and a systems subgroup of five. The whole project was modelled and managed in 3DEXPERIENCE.

I had two responsibilities. As a project manager I led the structural design team through the Preliminary Design Review and the Critical Design Review, ran the weekly progress meetings, and looked after configuration control, BOM generation and technical documentation in 3DEXPERIENCE, using its Gantt chart and issue and risk management widgets to track tasks, actions and decisions. As a structures engineer I owned the low speed aileron, one of the wing trailing edge devices, from loading actions through structural layout, detail sizing, FEA, lug design and fatigue assessment to the final CAD model.

The component

The low speed aileron sits on the outboard side of the wing and provides additional roll control at low speeds and altitudes, supplementing the high speed aileron so that roll performance is safe and effective in critical flight phases. It deflects up to plus or minus 20 degrees. Because no current certification standard covers a supersonic airliner on its own, the design had to meet EASA CS-25, which was never written for supersonic aircraft, alongside the older Concorde-era Transport Standard Specification (TSS).

Process

From loads to fatigue life

1
Loading actions

The structures subgroup split the loading actions between a symmetric team and an asymmetric team. I worked in the asymmetric team, which was responsible for roll performance and for the shear force, bending moment and torque under asymmetric load cases such as roll initiation, steady roll, roll arresting and roll reversal, as well as engine attachment loads. For the aileron itself I compared two-hinge, three-hinge and four-hinge arrangements, selected the three-hinge system because it distributed the loads more evenly with lower peak hinge loads, and produced the shear force, bending moment and torque diagrams along the span.

The CAD model then revealed a packaging problem. The outboard hinge had only 30 mm of depth available, not enough for an actuator, so the actuator had to move inboard to the middle hinge. This asymmetric actuator placement produces significantly more twist when the aileron is deflected, and that torque case drove the stiffness requirement for the whole structure.

2
Structural layout, materials and sizing

With the loads fixed I set out the initial structural layout, a single spar with ribs at the hinge and actuator stations, and sized the skin, ribs and spar from first principles using free body diagrams of the shear and axial loads, laminate force intensities and the ply proportions in each orientation. Material selection weighed the supersonic flight temperature as well as strength and stiffness, which is why the ribs and spar use 8552/HM63, the skin uses M21/IMA and the lugs use Al-7075. Manufacturing was considered at the same time, including the web to flange layup sequence, ply drop-offs and the filler at the trailing edge.

3
Finite element analysis in NASTRAN/PATRAN

Because of the aileron geometry the analytical laminate sizing was followed by a full finite element model in PATRAN, solved in NASTRAN. Skins, ribs and spar were modelled with shell elements and the rib flanges with beam elements, surfaces were equivalenced so that loads transfer cleanly between components, and the lugs were constrained at the hinge points. The pressure distributions for the subsonic case and the supersonic case (Mach 1.8) were applied separately. I then checked deformation, stress distribution, buckling and reserve factors across skins, ribs, spars and attachment lugs and iterated the layup until every component sat within its allowable limits.

4
Lug design and attachments

The aileron attaches to the wing through three hinges, two actuator lugs and one support lug. The lugs were sized in Al-7075-T6 using ESDU 91008 for the actuation point and ESDU 08007 for the hinge point, checking tension, shear tear-out and bearing failure under both axial and transverse loading, with reserve factors evaluated for ultimate and limit loads. The actuator lug went through three design iterations. The final solution kept the arrangement of the first design but changed the lug to a 3-2 configuration, which brought the force required from a single actuator down to about 146 kN, within the 155 kN capacity of the selected actuator. Because the lugs had to sit outside the thin aileron, I also designed fairings for them, taking the Concorde actuator fairing as the reference.

5
Fatigue and damage tolerance in AFGROW

Both lugs were then assessed for fatigue and damage tolerance in AFGROW. I built load spectra for the support lug, the actuator actuation point and the actuator hinge point from the TWIST stepped load spectrum and the repeated aileron deflections between neutral flight and full 20 degree deflection, ran the crack growth analysis and converted the results into the number of aileron deflections each lug can sustain. Both lugs have sufficient fatigue life for full operational use.

Outcome
  • Aileron mass of 53.87 kg, a 52% reduction against the baseline, with all strength margins and deformation limits satisfied and a maximum tip deformation of 18.2 mm
  • Structure meets the lateral stability requirements, withstands ultimate loads and has sufficient fatigue life under CS-25 with TSS as the supersonic reference
  • Final CAD model in 3DEXPERIENCE fitted entirely within the aerodynamic envelope supplied by the CAD team, with the mass breakdown handed to the Mass and CG team
  • Delivered and documented through PDR and CDR alongside my project management responsibilities for the structures team
PROJECT 02

Bird Strike Simulation on Fibre Metal Laminates

Comparative numerical simulation of bird strike on ARALL, CARALL and GLARE leading edge skins using Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics. MSc Individual Research Project, Cranfield University.

November 2025 to January 2026Supervisor: Dr. Ioannis GiannopoulosABAQUS/Explicit, SPH, Johnson-Cook, Hashin

Bird strike is a critical loading condition for the external skin of an aircraft during take-off and landing, and fibre metal laminates are used on leading edges because they resist impact well and slow crack growth in the metallic layers. This project compared three widely used fibre metal laminates under an identical bird strike so that the differences in the results could be attributed to the laminate systems alone.

I built the simulations in ABAQUS/Explicit with the bird modelled as a soft body using Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics, which captures the large deformation and fragmentation of the bird on impact, and the laminate skins modelled with finite elements. The aluminium layers use the Johnson-Cook ductile damage model and the composite plies use the Hashin failure criterion. The impact case follows CS-25.631: a 1.82 kg bird at 180 m/s. Geometry, laminate stacking, boundary conditions and impact velocity were kept identical for ARALL, CARALL and GLARE, and each system was assessed on the out-of-plane deformation of the skin and the internal energy absorbed during the impact.

What the results showed

  • CARALL limits deformation best, about 24% lower than GLARE, and ARALL deforms about 47% more than CARALL, because the IM7/8552 carbon plies have a much higher modulus than S2 glass or Kevlar 49. That stiffness comes with brittle behaviour: failed elements appear in the CARALL skin late in the impact, and once damage starts the laminate loses stiffness without absorbing much more energy
  • ARALL deforms the most but absorbs the most energy, 13.96 kJ against 5.14 kJ for CARALL, and it is also the lightest of the three, which makes it the most efficient energy absorber on a weight basis
  • GLARE sits between the two. It is the heaviest laminate, but it combines moderate deformation with progressive energy absorption through aluminium plasticity and composite damage, which matters under AMC 25.631 because lower skin deflection reduces the risk of secondary damage to ribs, spars and systems behind the leading edge. This balance explains why GLARE is so widely used on impact-exposed structures
  • Bird strike resistance is governed by the balance between stiffness, deformation control and energy dissipation, so deformation and energy based metrics should be used alongside penetration when comparing laminates at the preliminary design stage

Aerodynamic package

Front and rear wings

The wings had to be easy to assemble and disassemble while being as light as possible. Each wing element carries a different load, so each has its own layup. The main element uses one layer of carbon fibre, one layer of glass fibre and two further layers of carbon fibre, with 2x2 woven 220 GSM carbon and 2x2 woven 300 GSM glass throughout. Every element is made in a pair of male and female moulds coated with melamine for surface finish and release.

The structural analysis of the wings and wing mounts was run by other members of the composites team in ANSYS Composite PrepPost, under the downforce and drag loads supplied by the aerodynamics department, checking total deformation, safety factor and inverse reserve factor. I was involved throughout that work, and it set the laminate the parts had to be built to. My own contribution on the wings was the manufacturing: mould preparation, layup, vacuum bagging, trimming, closing the elements around the foam and aluminium ribs, and final assembly.

Bodywork and floor

The bodywork parts followed the same approach with layups chosen for each job. The floor closeout, which separates the driver from the track and carries the seat and steering rack, is a sandwich panel: two layers of carbon, one of glass, a 1 inch aluminium honeycomb core, then glass and two more layers of carbon, laid up on a waxed glass plate so that the panel comes out flat with a good surface finish. The side panels sit alongside the side impact structure between the front and main hoops. Across the bodywork this optimised design and material selection delivered a 25% weight saving.

Suspension

Suspension A-Arm structures

The A-Arms are the only load path between the wheel assembly and the chassis. I was given the hardpoint coordinates and the load cases by the vehicle dynamics department and designed the arms to those constraints in Fusion 360. Each arm is a three-piece assembly, an aluminium plate machined to the geometry of that corner bonded to two carbon fibre tubes.

The plates first went through a shape optimisation study with a mass to retain criterion of 60%, then the whole assembly, including the wheel upright so that the load path was realistic, was put through a static structural analysis in ANSYS. The loads correspond to 1.8 g of lateral acceleration against roughly 1.4 g that the car sees, with a minimum acceptable safety factor of 2, which together give the design a margin over anything the car will experience. The result was a 10% weight reduction while meeting the load requirements.
